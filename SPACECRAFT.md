## Saturn V

*The rocket that took humans to the Moon*

**Mission:** Apollo 11 — First humans on the Moon

| | |
|---|---|
| **Country** | United States |
| **Agency** | NASA |
| **Built by** | Boeing, North American Aviation & Douglas Aircraft |
| **First flight** | 1967 |
| **Status** | Retired (1973) |

### Quick Specs

| Spec | Value |
|------|-------|
| Height | 110.6 m |
| Mass | 2,970 t |
| Stages | 3 |
| Thrust | 35,100 kN |
| Payload to Moon | 48.6 t |

### Where it went

Low Earth orbit, then a trans-lunar trajectory to the Moon, ~384,000 km away.

### Why it went

The United States built Saturn V to win the Space Race and land astronauts on the Moon before the decade of the 1960s was out, as President Kennedy had promised.

### What happened

Apollo 4 proved the rocket unmanned in November 1967. Apollo 8 carried the first humans around the Moon in December 1968. On 20 July 1969, Apollo 11 landed Armstrong and Aldrin on the Sea of Tranquility. Five more crews landed on the Moon in subsequent missions. The final Saturn V launched the Skylab space station in May 1973.

On 20 July 1969 Apollo 11 delivered Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to the lunar surface — the first time humans walked on another world. Saturn V flew 13 times and never lost a crew. It remains the most powerful rocket to fly a completed operational program.

### Anatomy

1. **Launch Escape System** — A small tower rocket that could yank the crew capsule to safety in an emergency during launch.
2. **Command & Service Module** — The crew cabin and its support section — the only part that came home to Earth.
3. **Lunar Module** — Stored in a cone below the CSM; the spider-like craft that actually landed on the Moon.
4. **Third Stage (S-IVB)** — Fired twice: first to reach orbit, then again to push the spacecraft toward the Moon.
5. **Second Stage (S-II)** — Five J-2 engines burning liquid hydrogen to carry the stack toward orbital speed.
6. **First Stage (S-IC)** — Five giant F-1 engines burning kerosene and oxygen — 3.4 million kg of thrust at liftoff.

**Mission:** STS-1 & 135 flights building the ISS and launching Hubble

|------|-------|

### Where it went

Low Earth orbit, typically 300–600 km up.

### Why it went

NASA wanted a reusable vehicle that could carry large crews and cargo, deploy and repair satellites, and build a space station.

### What happened

Columbia flew the first mission in April 1981. Over three decades, five orbiters carried 355 people across 135 missions, building most of the ISS and deploying Hubble. Challenger was lost 73 seconds after launch on 28 January 1986. Columbia broke apart during re-entry on 1 February 2003. Atlantis flew the final mission in July 2011.

Over 30 years the Shuttle launched the Hubble Space Telescope, built most of the International Space Station, and flew 355 people. Its two tragedies — Challenger (1986) and Columbia (2003) — reshaped how the world thinks about spaceflight safety.

Low Earth orbit and geostationary transfer orbit; boosters return to land on a droneship or launch pad.

### Where it went

Intended for lunar orbit and a Soviet Moon landing that never happened.

### Why it went

The Soviet Union raced to beat America to the Moon, but attempted it with thirty first-stage engines that proved impossible to coordinate.

### What happened

All four launch attempts between 1969 and 1972 failed before the rocket cleared the lower atmosphere. All four N1 launches failed, one in the largest non-nuclear explosion in history. Its cancellation ended the Soviet crewed lunar program — but its NK-33 engines later flew on American rockets decades on.

## Hubble Space Telescope

*The telescope that rewrote the cosmos*

**Mission:** Deep views of the universe from orbit since 1990

| | |
|---|---|
| **Country** | USA / Europe |
| **Agency** | NASA / ESA |
| **Built by** | Lockheed & Perkin-Elmer |
| **Launched** | 1990 |
| **Status** | Operational |

### Quick Specs

| Spec | Value |
|------|-------|
| Mirror | 2.4 m |
| Orbit | ~540 km |
| Length | 13.2 m |
| Speed | 27,000 km/h |
| Servicing | 5 missions |

### Where it went

Low Earth orbit, above the blur of the atmosphere.

### Why it went

Astronomers wanted a telescope above the atmosphere to see the universe with unprecedented clarity across visible and ultraviolet light.

### What happened

Deployed from Space Shuttle Discovery in April 1990. A grinding error left the primary mirror too flat by 2.2 micrometres, producing blurred images. In December 1993, astronauts installed corrective optics during a demanding series of spacewalks. Five servicing missions over 19 years have kept it at the forefront of astronomy for more than three decades.

Hubble measured the age of the universe (~13.8 billion years), captured the famous 'Pillars of Creation', and its Deep Field images revealed thousands of galaxies in a patch of apparently empty sky. A daring 1993 spacewalk fixed its blurry mirror.

### Anatomy

1. **Aperture Door** — A lid that shields the delicate mirror from stray sunlight and space debris.
2. **Primary Mirror** — A 2.4-metre mirror, polished so smoothly its flaw was famously smaller than a wavelength of light — later corrected.
3. **Instrument Bay** — Cameras and spectrographs that split starlight into its colours to study distant galaxies.
4. **Solar Arrays** — Two wings of solar cells that power the telescope from sunlight.
5. **Fine Guidance Sensors** — Lock onto guide stars to hold the telescope rock-steady for long exposures.
6. **Communications Antennas** — Relay images down to Earth through NASA's tracking satellites.

### Where it went

Past Jupiter and Saturn, and since 2012 into interstellar space beyond the Sun's bubble.

### Why it went

A rare planetary alignment let one spacecraft tour multiple giant planets using their gravity to slingshot onward — a chance that comes once in 175 years.

### What happened
